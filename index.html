About the course

We interact with friends, teammates, and lawmakers online, and we read our news socially. These everyday activities are shaped by social computing systems—the platforms, algorithms, and interfaces that mediate our connections, determining whose content we see or who we should connect with. This course teaches how to design such systems to support activities ranging from collaboration and information sharing to deliberation, collective action, and beyond, as well as how to mitigate harms and risks to people who use our systems. We cover foundational concepts from the social sciences and explore how they apply to these systems, and we learn how to design experiments on social platforms and evaluate their outcomes.

What will we do in this class?

In lectures, I interleave social science literature that explains what we know about how humans think and act in groups with social computing research that examines how these tendencies emerge in online social spaces. We also learn how to design experiments that test hypotheses about these systems and how to evaluate the results. Together, these help us understand how to design systems that support the behaviors we want to foster while also mitigating those we want to discourage.

Each student will lead a short presentation of one paper and guide a short discussion on how it connects to course concepts and how its ideas might inform the design of our own systems.

There will be an in-class exam after the lectures on study design, data analysis, and causal inference, covering the material up to that point.

The group project challenges you to synthesize course lessons by creating a functional social computing system, such as a social platform, app, or browser extension, and deploying it to real users (friends, family, colleagues, etc.). This is a hard problem because it involves navigating many design decisions without a single “right” answer. You will apply concepts from the course to create joy and meaning in people’s lives while mitigating potential risks and harms. The project has four milestones, culminating in a paper on what you learned from designing your system, launching it to users, and analyzing user behavior.

Each milestone will include an in-class presentation by your group. The presentations given by other groups will serve as the source materials for your own assignments: at each milestone, you will write feedback for another team’s project, including what you liked about the project and why, what you wish were different, and recommendations for what the team might try next. These assignments are intended to 1) hone your reviewing skills (peer review being a fundamental bar for rigor and quality in the scientific community), and 2) help you stay engaged with and care about the progress of your classmates’ projects.

No textbook is required for this class. This course does not teach the programming skills required for development and deployment, so you should already be comfortable with these before enrolling.
